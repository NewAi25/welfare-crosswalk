# Which dairy welfare signs to push for when steering farm sensors

Manisha Sarkar. Sentient Futures incubator, mentor Kevin Xia. November 2026.

## The question

Farms can now buy sensors that watch cows, and welfare labels send inspectors to check how cows are kept. Kevin Xia's list of research projects asks for a crosswalk, a table lining up validated welfare indicators, what commercial sensors measure, and what certification schemes require and audit. In his words, the goal is to be able to say "these are the priority welfare indicators to push for when steering PLF". PLF is precision livestock farming: sensors and data analysis used on farms.

The full ranked list is in the [priority table](priority_table.md) and the evidence in the [Excel file](welfare_crosswalk_dairy.xlsx).

## What was done

The table has 81 rows. Fifty eight are measures from two welfare protocols: the 2023 opinion on dairy cows from EFSA, the European Food Safety Authority, and the Welfare Quality assessment protocol. The other 23 are things sensors on sale say they measure that no protocol covers, such as rumination (chewing the cud) or body temperature.

The sensor evidence starts from Stygar and colleagues 2021, a review that found 129 sensor technologies on the market, of which 18 had been externally validated, meaning tested on data from herds not used to develop them. Studies and makers' pages since 2021 sit beside that baseline. Four schemes were read in full against every row: RSPCA Assured, FARM version 5, Global Animal Partnership and Certified Humane.

Every value follows a written rule and names its source. A value the rules do not settle is marked unclear, never guessed. No welfare judgement was made.

## How the ranking works

An earlier version added five factors into a score. Kevin's review said the ranking was invalid once it held rows that are not welfare validated, and it caught FARM, the US dairy industry's own programme, being counted twice. Four ordered steps replace the score, drawn from his review and from a summary he forwarded of an exchange with Claude.

The first step is a gate. Five rows that the welfare sources read here do not tie to welfare leave the list. Rows whose link to welfare is questioned or unclear stay, with a flag.

The second step groups the rows into welfare states, so that three tests for one condition count once. There are 52 states, of which 47 pass the gate. Each carries forward its best test.

The third step ranks the states. First comes how well the best test did. The bar is the one Stygar and colleagues use for high performance, and every result a study reports must meet it: a correlation, which measures how closely two sets of readings move together, above 0.9, for example. Next comes the cheapest device the measure needs. Last comes how hard the measure is to take by hand, read from EFSA's feasibility ratings, because that is where a sensor adds the most.

The fourth step says what to ask for; the key under the results table explains each kind.

Of ten studies makers took part in, three count under a written conflict of interest test.

The list has no measure of how much each state matters to the cow. EFSA does not rank its welfare consequences, and Welfare Quality orders its criteria only within each of its four principles, so using either across this table would need a welfare judgement. The list says where the evidence and the equipment are ready, not which problem hurts cows most.

Passed and fell short refer to the bar described above. Flagged means the welfare sources question the link to welfare.

The main finding is a mismatch. On the ranked list, the five states whose sensors passed the bar are not ones any scheme is known to require, though for standing a scheme's wording may name it. The four states schemes do require, with a sensor on offer, have weaker evidence: subclinical ketosis cannot yet be graded, mastitis and lameness fell short of the bar, and feed availability is only claimed by a product.

Kevin's item names two valuable cells. The first, indicators already measured by sensors but required by no scheme, a set he said could be large, holds 21 rows, with 12 more unclear; three of the 21 are rows the welfare sources do not back. The second, indicators required by schemes but checked by hand, holds 24 rows. No scheme's numbered standards accept sensor data as the way an inspector checks any row.

Five things products measure are not backed by the welfare sources read here, among them individual water intake, which passed the bar.

For 20 states no product is graded as measuring the state.

## Recommendations

These do not weigh one welfare problem against another.

Start with the signals products already collect. Feeding time, feed intake and rumination all passed the bar, and no description in the 2021 product list says they are for welfare, though most naming rumination or feeding time list health or welfare beside them. One DeLaval page now says tracking rumination and eating time helps "overall cow health and welfare", so this gap may be closing. On farms that own the devices, alerts or thresholds would need new software, not new hardware. The table holds no cost data and no record of what farms own.

Next, ask schemes to adopt lying time, the one state in World A: add to schemes whose sensors passed the bar. EFSA rates it low on feasibility, so it is hard to take by hand. The other five states in that group need better evidence first: their sensors fell short of the bar or have no counted test.

Do not yet ask schemes to accept sensor data for what they already require. Ask first for independent validation against the bar. Lameness is the clearest case: all four schemes require it and check it by hand, so a sensor that passed would open the ask in every scheme. For subclinical ketosis, a 2025 test by ICAR, the International Committee for Animal Recording, of an analyser for milk fat and protein reports strong results, but does not say what its regression lines (trends fitted through its readings) plot, so it cannot yet be graded. The schemes also require a score of how much fat a cow carries, while the tested sensor reads milk.

Ask the schemes to clarify their wording for the eight states where a sensor exists and a rule may name the state but does not say so plainly.

Finally, where no product is graded as measuring the state, ask for new measurement, starting with what is closest. Social behaviour and comfort behaviour have been measured in cattle in research, but no product in the sources read offers them.

## What this does not show

The sensor evidence rests on a 2021 baseline and named sources since, not the whole market. What products do with a signal comes from their one line descriptions, and for two rows from makers' pages, not from farms. The welfare relevance of the sensor rows rests on three documents. Of the questions the rules could not settle, 54 remain open.

The checks were done by scripts and by an AI agent working from a checklist. No welfare scientist has reviewed the table. Poultry is future scope.
